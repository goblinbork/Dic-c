IV

Inversion

Avoid stupidity before you attempt brilliance.

Man muss immer umkehren. Invert, always invert.Attributed to Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi, 1804–1851

Month four

In 1986 Charlie Munger was asked to give the commencement speech at the Harvard School in Los Angeles, and instead of telling the graduates how to succeed he told them, in detail, how to guarantee a life of misery. Be unreliable. Learn only from your own experience and never from anyone else’s. Give up at the first setback. Think in a fog. He had borrowed the method from the nineteenth-century mathematician Jacobi, who solved hard problems by restating them backwards, and the speech is remembered when a thousand cheerful ones are forgotten because the backwards version is more useful. Everyone in the room already knew how to be unreliable. They had simply never heard it described as a strategy.

Inversion rests on an asymmetry that is easy to state and strange to accept. It is much easier to say what would wreck a life than what would perfect one, and the wrecking list is far more actionable, because every item on it is something you can stop. Nobody knows what makes a marriage great. Everybody knows what ends one. Medicine and aviation became safe not by discovering brilliance but by hunting down, one by one, the ways things go wrong.

So before this book asks you to build anything, it asks you to look for the failure. Not because failure is likely. Because it is knowable.

Why it works

In 1989 three researchers ran an experiment in which people were asked to explain a future event. Half were asked why it might happen. The other half were told it had happened and asked why. The second group produced about thirty per cent more reasons, and more specific ones. The psychologist Gary Klein turned this into a method he called the premortem: before a project begins, the team is told that it is a year later and the project has failed completely, and everyone writes down why. It works for two reasons. The certain past is easier to explain than the uncertain future, and the exercise gives everyone permission to say the thing they were going to be blamed for noticing later.

We need the help, because unaided we are absurdly optimistic. When students were asked how long their theses would take, they said thirty-four days on average, and forty-nine if everything went as badly as it possibly could. The actual figure was fifty-five. Fewer than a third finished by the date they had named. The error was not in the estimates of the work. It was in the failure to imagine the ways the work would be interrupted, which is exactly what inversion supplies.

It is easier to say what would wreck a life than what would perfect one, and far easier to act on.

Try it on a year rather than a project. How would you guarantee that the next twelve months were wasted? Keep the phone beside the bed. Say yes to everything so that nothing gets your full attention. Wait until you feel ready. Optimise for looking busy. Keep score against people who are not playing your game. Postpone the conversation, the doctor, the apology. Learn only from your own mistakes. Never write anything down. Most people, reading the list, recognise their current arrangements.

How to hold it

Three tools come out of inversion, and this month you will build all three. The premortem is the first. The second is the kill criterion, a phrase from the poker player Annie Duke: a signal, chosen in advance, at which you will stop. If by June I have not found ten paying customers, I close it. The point of choosing it now is that later you will be the wrong person to choose; you will be invested, tired, and telling a story. The person you are this month, who has not yet spent the year, is the only one who can be trusted to set the tripwire.

The third is the not-to-do list, which is the inverted goal. Goals are tiring; they require sustained effort against resistance. A not-to-do list requires only refusal, and refusal is available even on bad days. Five items, chosen from the wasted-year list, printed on the card, are worth more than most plans, because a plan tells you what to do when you are at your best, and a not-to-do list tells you what to do when you are not.

The institutions that have made the most progress with this instrument are the ones where failure is expensive. In 1935 the most advanced bomber in the world crashed on a test flight because a highly experienced crew forgot to release a gust lock, and the response was not more training. It was a card listing the things that had to be done before take-off, which every pilot in the world now reads aloud. Seventy years later, when hospitals in eight countries adopted a nineteen-item surgical checklist, complications fell by more than a third and deaths by nearly half. The list did not contain a single brilliant idea. It contained the known ways to fail, and the discipline of checking that none of them was happening today.

One caution. Inversion is a tool for avoiding ruin, not a temperament. People who only invert become careful and small. The next instrument is about the other half: once the downside is bounded, how to be bold. Do this one first, because you cannot take many bets if any one of them can end the game.

Practice · Instrument four

Run the premortem

Forty-five minutes · the most important thing you are doing this year · within the first seven days

  1. Name the thing. The project, the move, the relationship, the year itself. Write it at the top of a page.
  2. Declare the failure. Under it, write: It is [the date one year from today]. It has failed completely. Believe it for the next ten minutes.
  3. List the reasons. Every cause you can think of, specific, in the past tense. The first five will be obvious. Do not stop before fifteen.
  4. Circle the three most likely, and for each write one action this month that removes or weakens the cause. Put the actions in the calendar.
  5. Set the kill criterion. If by [date] [signal] has not happened, I stop. Write it on the ledger. It cannot be revised by the person you will be in June.
  6. Write the not-to-do list. Five things that would guarantee the wasted year, which you will not do. Copy them to the card.

Why it works Prospective hindsight produces more and better reasons than forecasting. Kill criteria move the decision to quit to the one moment you can make it honestly. A not-to-do list works on days when nothing else does.

Where it fails Listing causes you can blame on others. At least ten of the fifteen must be things you did or did not do.
