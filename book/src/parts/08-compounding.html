Instrument eight · Patience
VIII

Compounding

Small, repeated, for years, beats large, occasional, for months.

Play long-term games with long-term people.Naval Ravikant, 2018

Month eight

The arithmetic of compounding is usually presented as a motivational trick: get one per cent better every day and you will be thirty-seven times better in a year. The sum is correct and the premise is false, because nobody improves one per cent a day at anything for a year. The honest version is slower and more interesting. One per cent a week, which is achievable, makes you two thirds better in a year, which is unimpressive. It makes you thirteen times better in five years, and a hundred and seventy-six times better in ten. The curve is flat for a long time and then it is vertical, and the whole difficulty of compounding is that people leave during the flat part, because from inside the flat part it looks like nothing is happening.

Not everything compounds. Attention spent on the news does not; neither do credentials nobody checks, or work that leaves nothing behind when it is done. What compounds is anything that makes the next unit easier or more valuable than the last: a skill, a body of work, a reputation, a set of relationships built on kept promises, a body that is looked after, money that is left alone. And what compounds against you compounds just as reliably: debt, resentment, the unread messages, the back.

Why it works

The mechanism is mathematical and the obstacle is human. We discount the future steeply, and we discount our future selves most of all, treating the person who will be alive in ten years as a stranger whose problems are their own. The experiment mentioned in the first chapter bears repeating: shown an aged image of their own face, people more than doubled what they set aside for that person. The future self has to be made vivid before we will invest in it, and the way to make it vivid is to let it speak.

Two other ideas belong here. Cal Newport calls the stock of rare and valuable skills a person has built their career capital, and argues that the things everyone wants from work, autonomy and meaning and control over one’s time, are bought with it and with nothing else. And Naval Ravikant pointed out in 2018 that a single person now has access to forms of leverage that once needed an organisation: capital, other people’s effort, and, newest of all, code and media, products that cost nothing to copy and work while you sleep. What he called permissionless leverage has, since he wrote, grown by an order of magnitude. A person with a skill, a machine, and ten years is a different kind of thing than they were in any previous century.

The curve is flat for years and then it is vertical. Most people leave during the flat part.

The practical consequence is that the important question about any use of a week is not whether it is productive but whether it compounds. An hour that leaves nothing behind is an hour. An hour that adds to something is an hour plus a fraction of every hour that follows.

How to hold it

Choose one compounding asset. Not five: one. A skill, a body of work, a small set of relationships, a financial habit. The weekly non-negotiable you set in the second month is its vehicle, and the sacred hours of the seventh are its fuel. Then measure inputs, not outcomes, for as long as the curve is flat: weeks kept, not results achieved, because the results will not appear on any schedule you can plan for, and if you wait for them you will quit in year two, which is when almost everyone does.

The instrument this month is a letter. It is written by you, dated ten years from today, to the person you are now, and it describes what compounded: what the asset became, what it required every week, what it cost, what it made possible. Written honestly, the letter does two things. It makes the future self vivid enough to invest in. And it usually reveals, in the writing, that the weekly requirement is smaller than you feared, and the only hard part is not stopping.

Two illustrations, one famous and one useful. Warren Buffett has been investing since he was eleven and is one of the richest people who ever lived, and well over ninety-nine per cent of his fortune arrived after his fiftieth birthday. He was not much better at fifty than at forty. The curve had simply reached the vertical part. The useful illustration is a rule of thumb known as the Lindy effect: a thing that has lasted a long time can be expected to last about as long again. Choose assets accordingly. Clear writing, selling, listening, arithmetic and the ability to sit alone with a hard problem have compounded for people for centuries and will compound for you. The tool that was invented last year may or may not.

Charlie Munger’s first rule of compounding was never to interrupt it unnecessarily. The book cannot tell you what your asset is. It can only insist that you name one, protect it, and let the years do the arithmetic.

Practice · Instrument eight

The ten-year letter

Sixty minutes · by hand · kept inside this book · within the first seven days

  1. Name the asset. One skill, body of work, set of relationships or financial habit which, held for ten years, changes everything else. Write it on the ledger in one line.
  2. Write the letter. Dated ten years from today, from you to the person reading this now. One page. What compounded, what it demanded each week, what you gave up, what it made possible. Do not flatter yourself; the letter is more useful if it is plausible.
  3. Set the weekly minimum. The smallest unit you could sustain for five hundred and twenty weeks. Three hours. Five hundred words. One call. Write it and put it inside the non-negotiable from month two.
  4. Name what compounds against you, and start reducing it this month. Write the first step.
  5. Fold the letter and keep it at this page. The sealed envelope will ask about it.

Why it works A vivid future self more than doubles what people invest in it. Measuring inputs keeps you in the game through the flat years. A named asset with a weekly minimum is a compounding process; a vague ambition is not.

Where it fails Choosing three assets. Anything that competes for the same weekly hours halves the rate. One.
