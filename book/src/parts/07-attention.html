Month seven

A waking day is about a thousand minutes. Sixteen and a half hours, a little more or less depending on how the eleventh instrument is going, and then it is over and another begins. Ask most people where yesterday’s thousand went and they can account, with effort, for about seven hundred. The rest were spent, in the strict sense of the word, and nobody knows on what. That missing three hundred is three hundred a day, two thousand a week, a hundred thousand a year: two and a half months of waking life, every year, unaccounted for.

William James wrote that his experience was what he agreed to attend to, and he meant it literally. Attention is not a productivity technique. It is the material that a life is made of, because whatever you did not attend to, you did not experience, whoever you were with and wherever you were. This is why the phone matters. It is not that it wastes time. It is that it purchases, at a price of zero, the only thing you have, and it does so with the best engineering of the age. Herbert Simon saw this coming in 1971: a wealth of information, he wrote, creates a poverty of attention.

The instrument is a grid of a hundred boxes, ten minutes each, and it will tell you where the thousand go.

Why it works

The cost of divided attention is larger than it feels. In a series of experiments, Sophie Leroy showed that when people switch from one task to another, part of their attention stays behind on the first: she called it attention residue, and it measurably degrades performance on the second task. The residue is worst when the first task was left unfinished, and it shrinks when the person had reached a point of closure before switching. Every glance at a message is a switch, and every switch leaves residue, so a day of small interruptions is not a day with some minutes missing. It is a day in which every remaining minute was done worse.

The device does not even need to be used. Researchers who asked people to complete tests of working memory found that performance was lowest when their own phone was on the desk, higher when it was in a pocket or bag, and highest when it was in another room, even though in every condition it was switched off. The brain spends capacity on not looking at it. Willpower, in other words, is the wrong tool; distance is the right one. Environment beats intention, every day, for everyone.

Where the thousand minutes go is not a productivity question. It is the whole question.

One more finding cuts the other way. People given a boring, undemanding task during a break solved more creative problems afterwards than people who rested or worked hard. An attentive life is not a life of unbroken concentration; it needs empty time, walks without input, the shower and the queue. The phone has abolished those too. It is not only your focus it takes. It takes your boredom, which is where the ideas were.

How to hold it

Begin by counting. For three days, each evening, reconstruct the day in ten-minute boxes and put one word in each. Then colour the boxes you would choose again. The exercise is not to feel guilty; it is to see. Most people find that the unchosen boxes cluster in the same two or three places, and that they were not enjoying them.

Then choose the sacred hours: two hours a day, at the same time, for the work your governing lines require, with the phone in another room and one task on the desk. Not face down. Another room. Cal Newport calls this deep work, professional effort in a state of distraction-free concentration that pushes your abilities to their limit, and observes that it is rare, valuable and getting rarer. Two hours a day of it, for a year, is more than most people manage in a career. End each session by writing where you stopped and what comes next, because a task left with a known next step leaves less residue than one abandoned mid-thought.

Finally, remove one input for the month. One app, one feed, one source of news. Delete it, and on the last day, if you miss it, reinstall it. You will not. The boxes it occupied will fill with something, and the exercise is to notice what.

The scale of the problem is measurable. When a research firm installed a tool on volunteers’ phones to count every tap, swipe and click, the average user touched the device more than two and a half thousand times a day, and the heaviest users more than five thousand. Nobody chose that number. It is what a device optimised for touching produces when it is within reach, and it is why the instruction is a room, not a resolution. Call it the room rule.

One last observation, which belongs as much to the twelfth instrument as to this one. The violinists in the famous practice study could not manage more than about four hours of real concentration a day, and the best of them protected those hours and napped in the afternoon. Attention is finite, and what is left of it after the work is the part you give to other people. Simone Weil wrote that attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity. Most of us give the sacred hours to a screen and the residue to the people at dinner. The grid will show you whether that is true of you, and it is the one thing on it that is worth changing first.

Thought experiment

The invoice. Suppose your phone billed you ten dollars for every minute of attention and sent the invoice monthly. Estimate last month’s bill. Now consider that it was in fact paid, in the only currency you cannot earn more of, and that the invoice simply was not sent.

Practice · Instrument seven

The thousand minutes

Ten minutes each evening for three days · then two hours a day · begin within the first seven days

  1. Reconstruct three days in ten-minute boxes. Each evening, fill a hundred boxes with one word each: work, phone, child, walk, meeting, nothing. Use the grid on the ledger for the third day; plain paper for the first two.
  2. Colour the boxes you would choose again. Count the ones you cannot account for. Write the number.
  3. Set the sacred hours. Two hours, same time daily, one task, phone in another room. Write the hours on the ledger and defend them like a flight.
  4. Close every session. Before you stop, write one line: where I stopped, and the next step. This is what stops the residue following you into the evening.
  5. Remove one input for the month. Delete it today. Write what it was. On the last day, write what filled the space.

Why it works Counted minutes get chosen; uncounted minutes get taken. Distance from the device restores capacity that intention cannot. Closure removes residue. Empty time restores the incubation that ideas need.

Where it fails Sacred hours that move. If the hours change daily, they are not sacred; they are available, and something will take them.
