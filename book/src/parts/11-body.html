The mind is something the body does.

Month eleven

In 2003 a team at the University of Pennsylvania put forty-eight healthy adults in a laboratory for two weeks and allowed them four, six or eight hours in bed a night. The people on six hours, which is roughly what a great many working adults consider normal, declined steadily for the whole fortnight. By the end, their attention and working memory had degraded as much as if they had gone without sleep entirely for up to two nights. That is not the finding. The finding is in the sentence on the opposite page: they rated themselves only slightly sleepy, and were largely unaware of the deficit. The instrument that judges whether you are impaired is the one that is impaired.

Every instrument in this book runs on a brain, and a brain is something a body does. It is fed, slept, moved and lit, or it is not, and when it is not, the person carrying it does not feel stupid. They feel normal, and they make normal-feeling decisions that a rested version of them would not make. You are not tired. You are under-slept, and the difference is that tiredness is felt and under-sleep is not.

This chapter is not about health, in the usual sense. It is about judgment, and about the four cheapest ways to improve it that have ever been found.

Why it works

Sleep is the first, and the research above is enough. The second is movement. In a one-year trial, older adults assigned to walk briskly three times a week grew the memory-forming part of the hippocampus by about two per cent, reversing one to two years of ordinary age-related shrinkage, while the control group, who stretched, lost more than one per cent. The review literature is titled without irony: be smart, exercise your heart. Aerobic movement is the closest thing we have to a drug for attention, and it is free.

The third is light. The body’s clock is set by daylight in the morning, and a clock that is set runs everything else on time: alertness in the day, sleep at night, the hormones in between. Ten minutes outdoors within an hour of waking is the whole prescription, and most people, who now wake in dim rooms and look at bright screens at night, have it exactly backwards.

You cannot feel the deficit. That is the finding. The tired brain rates itself fine.

The fourth is breath, and here the evidence is newer. In 2023 a Stanford trial compared five minutes a day of several breathing patterns against five minutes of mindfulness meditation. Over a month, breathing produced the larger improvement in mood, and the most effective pattern was the simplest: a double inhale through the nose followed by one long exhale through the mouth, repeated slowly. It is the pattern the body already uses, spontaneously, before sleep and after crying. Five minutes of it lowers arousal reliably enough to be used before a decision, a conversation or a night’s sleep.

How to hold it

The four are not negotiable, which is the point: they are decided once and then not decided again. A fixed wake time, seven days a week, and an alarm for going to bed, not for getting up, set seven and a half hours before it. Daylight within an hour. Thirty minutes of movement a day, three of them hard enough to make conversation difficult. Five minutes of the breathing pattern when arousal is high or sleep is not coming. Written down, they take four lines. Kept for fourteen days, they change the quality of every other instrument in the book, because every other instrument is a form of judgment.

Two substances undo the first item more often than anything else. Caffeine has a half-life of around five to six hours, so a cup at four in the afternoon is half a cup at ten, still in the blood when the head is on the pillow. Alcohol in the evening brings sleep on faster and then fragments the second half of the night, which is where most of the useful sleep was. Neither has to be given up. Both have to be moved: caffeine to the morning, alcohol to the occasions rather than the evenings. And the seventh instrument applies here: the phone sleeps in another room, the bedroom is dark and cool, and the decision is made once, by arrangement, rather than nightly, by willpower.

Do not add to the list. There is an industry that would like to sell you a fifth, sixth and twentieth item, and most of them are at best harmless. The four above are the ones with evidence behind them that any adult can act on for nothing. If you have a condition, a clinician outranks this chapter; the chapter’s claim is modest and it is this. For most people, most of the time, these are the highest-return hours in the week, and the return arrives in the currency every other chapter spends.

The thirty minutes of movement need not be taken from the working day; they can be part of it. When Stanford researchers gave people tests of creative thinking sitting down and then walking, output rose by an average of sixty per cent on foot, and the effect persisted for a while after sitting back down. A walk is not a break from thinking. For a large class of problems it is the best available way of doing it, which is why so many of the people whose work lasted walked every day and said so.

Run it as a loop. Fourteen days, the grid on the ledger, and on the fifteenth day a written answer to a single question: what changed? The answer is usually not in the body. It is in the decisions.

Thought experiment

The impaired judge. Treat every decision you made after ten at night, or on fewer than six hours of sleep, as having been made by a slightly drunk colleague. Which of last month’s decisions were theirs? This is closer to measurement than metaphor: after seventeen to nineteen hours awake, performance matches a blood alcohol level of 0.05 per cent.

Practice · Instrument eleven

Fourteen days of the four

Four lines to decide · fourteen days to keep · begin within the first seven days

  1. Fix the wake time. Seven days a week. Count back seven and a half hours: that is the bedtime. Set the alarm for bed.
  2. Daylight within an hour of waking. Ten minutes outdoors, without sunglasses, whatever the weather. A window does not count.
  3. Move for thirty minutes a day, and three times this fortnight hard enough that talking is difficult. Walking counts. So do stairs.
  4. Breathe for five minutes whenever arousal is high or sleep will not come: two inhales through the nose, one long exhale through the mouth, slowly, for five minutes.
  5. Mark the grid every night, and on day fifteen write what changed in your decisions, your mood and the sacred hours.

Why it works Six hours a night produces impairment the impaired cannot detect. Aerobic movement grows the memory-forming brain. Morning light sets the clock that everything else runs on. Slow breathing lowers arousal faster than most alternatives. All four are free.

Where it fails Deciding daily. If the four have to be chosen each morning, they will lose to whatever is nearer. Decide once, in writing, and run the fortnight.
