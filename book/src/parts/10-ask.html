The Ask

Most of the world is a door that opens if you knock.

Plate X The bridge. New information lives across weak ties, and people who bridge groups have ideas that are judged better. Asking is cheaper than it feels: predictions of refusal run about double the truth. After Granovetter (1973), Burt (2004), Flynn & Bohns (2008).

Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you.Matthew 7:7

Month ten

The psychologist Richard Wiseman once gave a newspaper to people who described themselves as lucky and to people who described themselves as unlucky, and asked them to count the photographs in it. On the second page, in type two inches high, was a message: Stop counting. There are 43 photographs in this newspaper. The unlucky people took about two minutes. The lucky people took seconds. Further in was a second message, offering a cash prize to anyone who told the experimenter they had seen it. The unlucky group, heads down, counting, missed that too. It is a demonstration rather than a controlled study, but it illustrates what Wiseman found over a decade of interviews. Lucky people are not luckier. They notice more, they talk to more people, and they ask.

Asking is the cheapest bet in the fifth instrument’s table. The downside is a moment of discomfort, bounded and brief; the upside is a job, a mentor, a customer, an apology accepted, a door you did not know existed. And yet most people ask for almost nothing, because they are certain, without ever having tested it, that the answer will be no. The evidence says they are wrong, by about half.

Why it works

In a series of experiments at Columbia and Stanford, Francis Flynn and Vanessa Bohns asked people to predict how many strangers they would have to approach to get a given number to agree to a small request: fill in a questionnaire, walk them to a building, lend a phone. The predictions were about twice the real figure. People systematically underestimate how likely others are to say yes, because they focus on the cost of helping and forget that the person asked is also weighing the discomfort of refusing. Refusal is awkward. Most people would rather help.

The second finding is older and stranger. In 1973 Mark Granovetter asked professionals in Boston how they had found their jobs, and among those who had found them through a person, more than four in five had found them through someone they saw occasionally or rarely: a former colleague, a friend of a friend, a name from years before. Close friends know what you know. It is the weak ties, at the edges of your network, who know something you do not, and they will tell you only if you ask. Ronald Burt later measured what a bridge is worth: managers whose contacts spanned the gaps between groups had ideas that were judged more valuable, because a bridge shows options that nobody on either side can see.

The answer to the question you do not ask is always no, and it is the only no that is guaranteed.

There is even a finding that asking improves the relationship. In a 1969 experiment, people who were asked to do a favour for the experimenter afterwards liked him more than those who were not asked. Benjamin Franklin had described the effect two centuries earlier, having borrowed a rare book from a political rival in order to make a friend of him. We like the people we have helped, because we have to explain to ourselves why we helped them.

How to hold it

Ask well. A good ask is specific, small enough to say yes to in a minute, and comes with a way out: no is completely fine. It says who you are in one line, what you have done that is relevant in one line, and what you want in one line, and it stops. It does not apologise for existing. And it asks for the thing itself, not for permission to ask for it later, because could I pick your brain sometime is a request for a meeting about a request.

Asking upward has its own grammar. The people whose help would change things receive many requests and answer the ones that show work: I read the thing you wrote, I tried what it suggested, this is what happened, here is my one question. Effort is the currency, and a specific question that took you an hour to earn is answered more often than a general one that took a minute. And when the answer is no, the relationship is almost never damaged by it. People remember being asked well. What they remember badly is being asked vaguely, or not at all, by someone who later turned out to have needed them.

Then change what you count. In 2012 a man named Jia Jiang spent a hundred days making requests designed to be refused, to cure himself of the fear of rejection, and found that a surprising number were accepted and that the refusals, once collected deliberately, stopped hurting. Collect the no’s. Set a number for the month, ten, and go and get them. You cannot reach ten no’s without collecting several yeses on the way, and by the tenth no the instrument has done its work, because the thing you were afraid of has happened repeatedly and you are fine.

When Steve Jobs was twelve he wanted to build a frequency counter and needed parts, so he looked up Bill Hewlett, the founder of Hewlett-Packard, in the telephone directory and called him at home. Hewlett talked to him for twenty minutes, gave him the parts, and gave him a summer job. Jobs told the story for the rest of his life, and the moral he drew was not about Hewlett. Most people, he said, never pick up the phone and call. Most people never ask. And that, sometimes, is what separates the people who do things from the people who dream about them.

One ask is different from the others, and it is on the practice page. There is a letter you have been not writing. Everyone has one: the thanks never given, the apology, the request to the person whose help would change things. Write it this month, by hand, and send it. It is the highest-asymmetry bet in the book.

Thought experiment

The two lists. Write the five people who would say yes to you without hesitation. Now write the five you would hesitate to ask. The first list is where the comfort is. The second is where the information is, and it is the one this month is about.

Practice · Instrument ten

Five asks in seven days

Twenty minutes to list · seven days to ask · one letter · begin within the first seven days

  1. List ten asks you have been avoiding. A person, a thing, a sentence. Introductions, rates, help, forgiveness, a seat at a table.
  2. Rank them by asymmetry. Bounded downside, unbounded upside first. Pick five.
  3. Make the five within seven days. Specific, short, with a way out. Write on the ledger what the answer was, not what you feared it would be.
  4. Write the letter you have been not writing. Five hundred words, by hand, to the person it is for. Send it or deliver it. Write on the ledger to whom, and when.
  5. Collect ten no’s before the month ends. Tally them. Note what each one taught you about how to ask.

Why it works People underestimate compliance by about half; information lives in weak ties; being asked makes people like you more, not less; deliberately collected refusals stop hurting. Every ask is a bet with bounded downside.

Where it fails Asking vaguely, asking for permission to ask, and asking only people who cannot say no. If all five were easy, they were not the five.
