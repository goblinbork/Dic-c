The Loop

You become whatever you get fast feedback on.

Plate VI Loop length. The time between an action and an honest signal sets how many lessons a year can hold. Cut the longest loop by ten.

The first principle is that you must not fool yourself – and you are the easiest person to fool.Richard Feynman, Caltech, 1974

Month six

A twelve-year-old will learn a difficult video game in a week and fail to learn long division in a year, and the difference is not motivation. In the game, every action is followed within a second by an honest signal about whether it worked. In the classroom, the signal arrives in a fortnight, in red ink, about something the child no longer remembers doing. Call the time between an action and an honest signal about it the loop length. It predicts how fast a person learns better than anything else we can measure, including the thing we call talent.

Most adult skills have loops measured in months or years. You give the presentation and never learn what the room thought. You make the hire and find out in a year. You write in the way you have always written because nobody has ever told you, specifically and soon, where a reader got lost. In such conditions people do not improve with experience; they simply repeat their first year many times and become confident.

This instrument is a way of taking the loops that matter and making them short.

Why it works

The research on expertise is usually summarised as ten thousand hours, which is a misreading of a 1993 study of violinists that its own author spent the rest of his life correcting. What the study actually found was that the best players had spent more time in a particular kind of practice: designed, effortful, aimed at a specific weakness, with immediate feedback. Hours alone predicted little. A later meta-analysis found that deliberate practice explained about a quarter of the variation in performance in games and a fifth in music, but only four per cent in education and less than one per cent in the professions. That last figure is often quoted as evidence that practice does not matter at work. Read it the other way. Games and music have loops of seconds. The professions have loops of years, so long that practice cannot find anything to grip.

The best-studied loop is the smallest. Students who learned a list of foreign words and then repeatedly tested themselves recalled about eighty per cent a week later. Students who spent the same time re-reading the list recalled about a third. Re-reading feels like learning and is not; the test, which feels like failure, is the learning. Spacing the tests out over days beats cramming them, by a wide margin, in every study that has looked. And the forecasters in Philip Tetlock’s tournaments who beat professional analysts did it mostly by keeping score, updating often, and in small steps. They had closed a loop that their rivals had left open.

Talent is a rumour. Loop length is a measurement.

How to hold it

A loop has three parts, and each can be shortened. The action can be made smaller: a paragraph reviewed daily teaches more than a chapter reviewed monthly, because it is reviewed thirty times. The signal can be made sooner: show the draft, the prototype, the plan, before it is finished, to someone whose job is not to like you. And the signal can be made honest: a count of readers is a signal, a count of likes is not; a friend’s praise is not, a stranger’s confusion is. Feedback that is fast but flattering is worse than none, because it trains you confidently in the wrong direction.

Two habits follow. The first is to test yourself instead of re-reading, which applies to this book: on the last day of each month, before opening the chapter, write from memory what the instrument said. Then check. What you could not retrieve, you had not learned. The second is to keep score in writing, which is why every ledger in this book ends with a signature and a date. A record you have to sign is a loop closed.

Two cautions. The first is that a measure, once it becomes a target, stops measuring: the economist Charles Goodhart noticed this in banking and it holds everywhere, from word counts to step counts. When the number starts to feel like the point, change the number. The second is that the fastest honest loop is almost always a person. A coach, an editor, a customer, a child who does not understand your explanation. Software can tell you what happened; only a person can tell you what it was like, and it is usually what it was like that you needed to know.

The surgeon Atul Gawande, eight years into practice and with his results plateaued, did something surgeons do not do: he asked a retired professor to stand in his operating theatre and watch. The first session produced a page of notes on things Gawande had not known he was doing, from where he stood to how he held the light, and over the following months his complication rate, which had stopped improving, began to fall again. Every performer with a coach has a loop measured in minutes. Almost everyone else, once the formal training ends, has a loop measured in years, and calls the resulting silence experience.

The instrument this month is arithmetic. Find the longest loop attached to something your governing lines require, and cut it by ten. If you learn how the meeting went once a quarter, learn it weekly. If you find out whether the writing works once a year, find out on Friday. Call the number of honest iterations a year the loop ratio; it is the one statistic of learning you control directly. The rate at which you become the person in the eulogy is set by the length of these loops, and almost all of them are longer than they need to be.

Thought experiment

The silent year. Suppose that for a year nobody gave you any feedback at all, except one person of your choosing, once a week, for ten minutes. Who would you choose, and what would you show them? That person is the loop you should have already, and the cost of asking them is one email.

Practice · Instrument six

Shorten the loop

Thirty minutes to design · thirty days to run · begin within the first seven days

  1. Name the three skills your governing lines require. Not roles: skills. Writing clearly, running a room, staying calm with a child, selling, listening.
  2. Measure the loop. For each, write how long currently passes between doing it and knowing honestly how it went. Days, weeks, never.
  3. Take the longest and design a loop ten times shorter. Who gives the signal, in what form, how often. A person, a test, a number, a recording of yourself. Write the design on the ledger.
  4. Run it for thirty days. Mark each iteration on the grid. Aim for thirty. Change the design on day ten if it is not producing honest signal.
  5. Retrieve, do not re-read. On the last day of every month from now on, write what the chapter said before you open it. Then open it and grade yourself.

Why it works Testing beats re-reading by a factor of two in retention; spacing beats massing; fast honest signal is what deliberate practice is made of. Shortening a loop by ten multiplies the iterations in a year by ten.

Where it fails Choosing a signal that is fast and kind. If the loop never tells you anything you did not want to hear, it is not a loop. It is applause.
