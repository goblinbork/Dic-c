The Others

Good relationships keep us happier and healthier. Period.

Plate XII The layers. Human networks come in rings of roughly five, fifteen, fifty and a hundred and fifty. The inner five carry the health effect. After Dunbar; the number is disputed, the layer is not.

The clearest message that we get from this 75-year study is this: Good relationships keep us happier and healthier. Period.Robert Waldinger, director, Harvard Study of Adult Development, 2015

Month twelve

In 1938 researchers at Harvard began following two groups of young men: two hundred and sixty-eight sophomores from the college, and four hundred and fifty-six boys from the poorest neighbourhoods of Boston. Seven hundred and twenty-four people, tracked through the war, their marriages, their careers, their illnesses and their deaths, and now their children. It is the longest study of adult life ever conducted, and its current director, Robert Waldinger, was asked, after seventy-five years of data, what it had found. He said it in two sentences, and the second one was Period.

What predicted health at eighty was not cholesterol at fifty. It was how satisfied people were, at fifty, with their relationships. The lonely declined earlier, in body and in memory, and the well-connected, whatever their income and whether or not their marriages were peaceful, were protected. Bronnie Ware’s dying patients said the same thing from the other end: they wished they had stayed in touch with their friends, and they wished they had said what they felt.

Eleven instruments have been about you. This one is about who you become with, and it is last because the others make it possible: you now have weeks that are counted, asks that are made, and a body that can carry you to people.

Why it works

Relationships behave like the body: they need maintenance and they decay silently. Waldinger and his co-author call it social fitness, and the phrase is exact. Nobody decides to lose a friend. The weeks are simply spent elsewhere, one at a time, and the friendship is discovered to be gone only when it is needed. Robin Dunbar’s work on the size of human networks suggests they come in layers of roughly five, fifteen, fifty and a hundred and fifty, and that the inner five, the people you would call at three in the morning, get the largest share of the time we have for others. The number is disputed. The layer is not. Everyone knows who their five are, and almost everyone, asked when they last spoke to each of them properly, is embarrassed by the answer.

One intervention has been tested more than any other. In 2005 Martin Seligman and colleagues randomised people to a set of exercises. In one, participants wrote a letter of gratitude to someone who had changed their life and never been properly thanked, and delivered it in person, reading it aloud. It produced the largest immediate rise in happiness of any exercise in the study, and the effect was still measurable a month later. A second exercise, writing down three good things that had happened each day and why, was slower to act and lasted six months.

The people who were most satisfied in their relationships at fifty were the healthiest at eighty.

And generosity, which sounds like a cost, turns out to be a strategy. Adam Grant’s research on givers, takers and matchers found givers over-represented at the bottom of every success ranking and also, unexpectedly, at the top. The difference between the two groups was not how much they gave but whether they had boundaries. The successful givers gave in ways that did not consume them.

Beyond the five there is the wider ring, and it matters more than it seems. When Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn had people count their daily interactions, those who had more exchanges with acquaintances, the barista, the neighbour, the colleague from another floor, reported belonging more and feeling happier on those days. The weak ties that brought information in the tenth instrument also carry mood. A life with a strong five and no wider ring is protected but narrow; the ring is cheap to maintain and it is where the new people come from.

How to hold it

Name the five. Beside each name write the date of the last real conversation, and look at the column. Then take one weekly call from the non-negotiable hours: one of the five, half an hour, every week, for the rest of the year. It is the cheapest instrument in the book and, on the study’s evidence, the one with the longest reach.

Write the gratitude letter and deliver it in person. Three hundred words, specific, about what the person did and what it changed. Read it to them. It is uncomfortable for about ninety seconds and then it is one of the best hours either of you will have this year. And repair one thing. There is a conversation you have been postponing; the fourth instrument told you what postponing costs. Have it this month.

On repair, the research is unusually practical. John Gottman, who watched couples in his laboratory for decades, found that the ones who stayed together were not the ones who argued least. They kept a ratio of about five positive exchanges to every negative one, and they answered small bids for attention, a remark about the weather, a hand on the shoulder, by turning toward the person rather than away, most of the time. Call it the small turn. The couples who later separated turned toward each other about a third of the time. Relationships are not maintained by grand gestures. They are maintained by the small turn, repeated, and it is the cheapest habit in this book.

The eulogy in the first chapter had a speaker. Go back and look at who you chose. They are on the list of five, or they should be, and the whole year has been, in the end, a way of deserving what you hoped they would say.

Thought experiment

The three a.m. list. Write the people you would call at three in the morning. Then write the people who would call you. If the two lists differ, the difference is the work of this month, and probably of the next year.

Practice · Instrument twelve

The gratitude visit

Twenty minutes to name · sixty minutes to write · one visit · begin within the first seven days

  1. Write the five names. The people whose presence in your eulogy you would most want. Beside each, the date of the last real conversation.
  2. Write a gratitude letter. Three hundred words to one person who changed your life and was never properly thanked. Specific: what they did, what it changed.
  3. Deliver it in person, and read it aloud. If distance makes that impossible, by call, with the camera on. Write on the ledger when.
  4. Schedule the weekly call. One of the five, thirty minutes, same slot each week, for the next twelve weeks. Mark the grid.
  5. Repair one thing. The conversation you have been postponing. Have it this month. Write what it was, not what was said.

Why it works Relationship satisfaction at fifty predicts health at eighty better than any biomarker. The gratitude visit is the fastest-acting exercise in the literature. A weekly call is social fitness made into an appointment, which is the only form in which it survives.

Where it fails Sending the letter instead of reading it. The reading is the mechanism. And choosing five people who are all easy: at least one should be someone you have been avoiding.
