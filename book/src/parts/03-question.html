III

The Question

The quality of a life follows the quality of its questions.

Be patient toward all that is unsolved in your heart and try to love the questions themselves. Live the questions now.Rainer Maria Rilke, Letters to a Young Poet, 1903

Month three

In 1986 the mathematician Richard Hamming gave a talk at Bellcore called You and Your Research, and the part people remember is a pair of questions he used to put to colleagues at lunch. What are the important problems in your field? And why aren’t you working on them? He was not, he reported, welcome at that table afterwards. The questions were rude only because they were good. A good question is a search image: once you have it, you cannot stop seeing what it points at, and you cannot un-see what it makes obvious.

We are now surrounded by machines that will answer almost any question in seconds. This changes something fundamental. For most of history, the answer was the expensive part: it took a library, an expert, an experiment. Now the answer is nearly free, and the question is the only scarce input left. A person who asks better questions than the people around them has an advantage that compounds, because the answers arrive at the same speed for everyone.

This instrument is a way of generating questions on purpose, and of finding, among them, the one that matters.

Why it works

Questions direct attention, and attention, as the seventh instrument will argue, is the substance of experience. But there is a more specific finding. In the 1960s Jacob Getzels and Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi watched art students in a studio, given a table of objects and asked to produce a drawing. Some students picked up two objects and began. Others handled far more of them, rearranged them, changed their minds, and started late. The second group produced work judged more original, and when the researchers followed the students for years afterwards, it was the problem-finders, not the fast starters, who had become the successful artists. The quality of the drawing had been decided before the pencil touched the paper, in the choice of what to draw.

Most of us are fast starters. We take the first framing of a problem, usually the one somebody else handed us, and work hard inside it. The question how do I get promoted produces a different decade from what work would I do if nobody could see me do it, and both are answerable. The framing is where the leverage is, and framing is a question.

Answers are now cheap. The question is the only scarce part.

There is also a structural reason to hunt for questions in bulk. The first ones you write are the ones you have already been asked. It takes twenty or thirty to exhaust the obvious, and it is only after that, when the easy material is gone, that a question appears that you have never let yourself ask. Every person who has done the exercise on the next page reports the same shape: the first twenty are dull, the next twenty hurt, and somewhere in the last ten is the reason they bought the book.

How to hold it

Among the questions, look for one with a particular property: its answer settles the others. Engineers borrow a word from mathematics and call it the eigenquestion. Should I take the job may be settled by do I want to be the kind of person that job produces, which is settled by the eulogy. When you find the eigenquestion you will know, because the list gets shorter as you look at it.

A few forms are reliably useful. What would have to be true for this to be the right choice? turns an argument into a list of checkable facts. What is the smallest experiment that would settle this? turns a worry into a week. Whose approval am I optimising for? is usually answered by a name, and the name is usually a surprise. What do I already know that I am pretending not to know? is the one that hurts, and it is the one to carry. And for anything you have been calling complicated: if I had to decide by Friday, what would I decide? Most complicated things are simple things we would rather not say aloud.

Two older forms deserve a place. The engineers at Toyota asked why five times in a row, each answer becoming the next question, until a broken machine had been traced to a filter nobody had thought to clean. It works on lives as well as on factories: ask why you are tired five times and you rarely end up at sleep. And Paul Graham, writing about how great work gets done, reduced the search for a vocation to one question: what are you excessively curious about, curious to a degree that would bore most other people? He added that curiosity never lies. Neither of these is a trick. They are questions shaped so that the honest answer is the only one that fits.

Leonardo da Vinci’s notebooks are full of lists of questions, and they are the most human thing in them. Describe the tongue of the woodpecker. Ask Benedetto Portinari how they walk on ice in Flanders. Ask the master of arithmetic to show you how to square a triangle. He did not have answers to most of them, and he wrote them down anyway, because a question written down goes on working when the mind that wrote it has moved on. Keep yours in the commonplace book that the ninth instrument will ask you to start, and read the list once a month. Some of the questions will have answered themselves. Others will have become the work.

Do not answer the questions in the sitting. The exercise is to generate them and to sit with them, in Rilke’s sense, for a month. Write the one you choose on the card and carry it. Questions carried for a month tend to answer themselves, quietly, usually while you are doing something else.

Practice · Instrument three

Fifty questions

Sixty minutes · one sitting · a second sitting the next day · within the first seven days

  1. Write fifty questions about your own life. Numbered, one sitting, no editing and no answering. Anything is admissible: work, money, the body, the people, the things you avoid. Do not stop before fifty. The first twenty are for warming up.
  2. The next day, read them cold. Circle the three that made you uncomfortable. Discomfort is the signal; it means the question is live.
  3. For each of the three, write the answer you are pretending not to know. One sentence each. If the sentence is easy, you circled the wrong question.
  4. Find the eigenquestion. Among the fifty, which single question, if answered, would settle most of the others? Write it on the ledger and on the card.
  5. Carry it for the month. Do not force an answer. Note on the ledger, on the last day, what it turned into.

Why it works Problem-finding, not problem-solving, is what separated the artists who lasted from the ones who did not. Bulk generation exhausts the borrowed questions and exposes your own. A carried question recruits attention for a month at no cost.

Where it fails Stopping at thirty. Answering as you go. Choosing a comfortable eigenquestion. If none of the fifty frightened you, write fifty more.
