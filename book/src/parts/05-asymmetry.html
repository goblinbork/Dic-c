Instrument five · Risk
V

Asymmetry

Make many small bets whose downside is bounded and whose upside is not.

Most decisions aren’t like that – they are changeable, reversible – they’re two-way doors.Jeff Bezos, letter to shareholders, 2016

Month five

In his 2015 letter to shareholders, Jeff Bezos divided decisions into two kinds. Some are consequential and irreversible: one-way doors, to be taken slowly, with deliberation and consultation, because if you walk through and do not like what you see, you cannot get back. But most decisions are not like that. They are changeable, reversible, two-way doors, and they should be made quickly, by individuals or small groups, because the cost of a wrong two-way decision is small and the cost of slowness is not. His complaint was that large organisations use the heavy process on everything. So do people. We agonise over two-way doors as if they were one-way, and then, exhausted, walk through the one-way doors without looking.

Reversibility is the first sort, and it is quick: can I undo this in a month at a price I would pay? If yes, decide today. The second sort is the one this chapter is about, and it decides a life. For every choice, ask two questions. What is the worst that can happen, and is it bounded? What is the best, and is it?

Sending the letter, publishing the essay, asking the stranger, starting the small thing on Saturday mornings: the worst case is an hour and some embarrassment, and the best case has no ceiling. The shortcut, the lie, the uninsured risk, the borrowed money: the best case is a little more of what you already have, and the worst case is the end of the game. These are not the same kind of choice, and almost nobody treats them differently.

Why it works

The outcomes that shape a life are not evenly distributed. A career is made by one or two opportunities, a body of work by a handful of pieces, a life by a few people. The psychologist Dean Simonton found that across creative careers the ratio of hits to attempts is roughly constant: the most prolific creators produce the most masterpieces because they produce the most of everything, including failures. If you cannot know in advance which attempt will matter, the only strategy is many attempts, each cheap enough to survive.

But this only works if no single attempt can remove you from the game. Ruin is not a large loss; it is the loss after which there are no more bets. So the order matters, and it is the order of this book: bound the downside first, with the inversion you did last month, and then be as bold as the bound allows. The people who seem reckless and keep winning are usually not reckless. They have simply made sure that nothing they try can kill them, and then tried everything.

How to hold it

The enemy of this instrument is a habit the poker player Annie Duke calls resulting: judging a decision by its outcome. A good bet that lost was still a good bet; a bad bet that won is still a bad bet, and the win will teach you to make it again. The only defence is to record the decision at the time it is made, before the outcome exists. Peter Drucker recommended exactly this: whenever you make a key decision, write down what you expect to happen, and nine or twelve months later compare. He said he had done it for twenty years and that it was the only reliable way he knew to find out what he was actually good at. Benjamin Franklin, in 1772, described dividing a sheet into two columns, pro and con, adding to it over several days, and striking out reasons of equal weight until the balance showed. He called it moral algebra. It is the oldest decision instrument in this book, and the ledger opposite is its descendant.

For the rare one-way door, borrow a question from the third instrument: what would have to be true for this to be right? Then find the cheapest way to check each thing before you walk through. Most one-way doors have a two-way door standing next to them: a trial, a sabbatical, a smaller version, a conversation. Take that one first.

The mathematics of this was worked out at Bell Labs in 1956 by John Kelly, who showed that a bettor with an edge should stake a fixed fraction of their bankroll, never the whole of it, and that the fraction which grows wealth fastest is also the one that makes ruin impossible. Ed Thorp used the formula at blackjack tables and then in the markets for thirty years. Venture investors use a cruder version of the same idea: a fund makes twenty bets expecting most to fail, because one or two will return more than all the rest together, and the only unforgivable error is to be unable to make the twenty-first. A life is a fund of this kind. The people who look lucky are the ones who were still solvent when their bet came in.

And size the bets. You do not need the mathematics of the Kelly criterion to keep its spirit: never stake the amount, of money or time or reputation, that would make the next bet impossible. Small, many, survivable, and let the arithmetic of the tail do the rest.

Practice · Instrument five

Sort the bets

Sixty minutes · then ten minutes each week · within the first seven days

  1. List every decision you have been putting off. Ten minutes, no filter. Include the small ones; they are the ones that have been costing you.
  2. Mark each door. One-way or two-way: can it be undone in a month at a price you would pay? Decide every two-way door today, and write the date you will review it.
  3. For each one-way door, write what would have to be true for it to be right, and the cheapest way to find out. Put the finding-out in the calendar.
  4. List ten small bets. Bounded downside, unbounded upside: the letter, the essay, the ask, the small thing on Saturday. Place three this month.
  5. Open the decision ledger. For every significant decision this month, write what you expect to happen and a date to check. Review the entries in nine months. This is the one habit on the page that should outlive the book.

Why it works Reversible decisions made fast free the attention that irreversible ones need. Hits scale with attempts when no attempt can end the game. A written expectation is the only cure for resulting.

Where it fails Treating a wager as a bet. If the downside is your savings, your health or your name, it is not small, however good the story.
