Instrument nine · Creativity
IX

Taste

Quantity is the road to quality.

Plate IX The gap. Taste arrives before ability and is what makes early work disappointing. The only known way across is volume. After Ira Glass (2009).

To create consists precisely in not making useless combinations and in making those which are useful and which are only a small minority. Invention is discernment, choice.Henri Poincaré, Science and Method, 1908

Month nine

There is a story in a small book called Art & Fear about a ceramics teacher who divided a class in two. One half would be graded on quantity: fifty pounds of pots for an A, forty for a B. The other half would be graded on quality, and needed to produce only one pot, but a perfect one. At the end of the term, the best pots all came from the quantity group. They had made hundreds, learned from each, and got better. The quality group had sat theorising about perfection and produced, in the end, a great deal of theory and some mediocre clay. The story is a parable; one of the authors later admitted it was borrowed from a photography class and the medium changed. But the finding it dramatises is real and has been measured.

Dean Simonton, who has spent a career studying creative output, found what he called the equal-odds rule: across a career, the ratio of a creator’s hits to their total output stays roughly constant. Quality does not rise as a fraction of quantity. The people who produced the most masterpieces were the people who produced the most work, and they also produced the most failures, because the failures and the masterpieces come from the same process. There is no known way to make only the good ones.

This is the instrument for anyone who has ever wanted to make something and found that what they made was not as good as what they could see. That gap has a name.

Why it works

Ira Glass gave it the name. Everyone who does creative work, he said, gets into it because they have good taste. But for the first years the work is not as good as the taste, and the taste, which is what brought you in, is exactly what makes the work disappointing. Most people quit in that gap. The only way through it is a volume of work. Taste, in other words, is what you have when your standards outrun your hands, and the cure is not better standards.

Poincaré described creation as combination followed by choice. Invention, he said, is discernment among combinations, most of which are useless. Both halves need volume. Combination needs raw material, which is why every creative tradition has kept a commonplace book: John Locke published a method for one in 1706, Leonardo filled thousands of pages, and the emperor Marcus Aurelius wrote the notes to himself that we call the Meditations with no intention of anyone reading them. And choice needs candidates. You cannot discern among three ideas. You can among a hundred.

Taste is what you have when your standards outrun your hands. The only known cure is volume.

There is one more reason quantity works, and it connects to the sixth instrument. Each piece made and shown is a loop closed. The person who makes fifty small things in a year has had fifty honest signals. The person who spent the year on one large thing has had none, and has also, somewhere around month eight, started to protect it from the signal it needs.

The history of the people we call geniuses is mostly a history of volume. Edison was granted more than a thousand patents, most for things nobody remembers. Bach wrote well over a thousand works, many of them for the following Sunday. Picasso left tens of thousands of pieces. We remember the hits and forget that the hits were a small, fixed fraction of a very large number, and that the people who made them had no better idea than you do which ones would last.

How to hold it

Separate generation from judgment. Never edit in the same sitting as you produce; the critic and the maker are different people and they should not be in the room together. Generate in bulk, on a timer, and judge tomorrow. Ship small things on a schedule, weekly if you can, and show each one to somebody outside your house. Keep the commonplace book: one page a day, in your own hand, of sentences, ideas and observations that struck you, because the combinations that will matter in year three are made of what you collected in year one.

Use constraints on purpose. A form, a word count, a deadline, a single colour, a fixed hour. The blank page is not freedom; it is the absence of a problem, and problem-finding, as the third instrument said, is where the work is. And watch the ratio between consuming and making. For most people it is twenty to one, and no amount of taste survives it. This month the ratio is one to one: an hour made for every hour consumed.

Montaigne had the beams of his library painted with sentences from the books he loved, so that he could not look up from his desk without meeting one, and the essays he wrote in that room, which invented a form, are made largely of those sentences argued with. That is what the commonplace book is for. It is not an archive. Read it once a month and the entries begin to combine, and the combinations are the ideas that you will later believe you had.

And do not polish the first version. Make the second. Almost everything that is good about a piece of work arrives in the rewrite, when the maker can finally see what the thing wanted to be, and almost everything wrong with a first draft is fixed faster by a second draft than by any amount of attention to the first. This is quantity again, at the scale of a single piece.

Finally, do not wait to be good. The gap does not close by waiting; it closes, in Glass’s words, by going through a volume of work, and there is no way to get to the other side except through the middle.

Thought experiment

The hundred drafts. If you were required to produce a hundred versions of your next piece of work before choosing one, how would your process change today? Most of those changes are available at ten versions, and nearly all of them at two.

Practice · Instrument nine

One hundred ideas

Ninety minutes in one sitting · then one small thing a week · begin within the first seven days

  1. Write one hundred ideas. For the work your governing lines require: projects, essays, products, experiments, gifts, questions, changes. Numbered, one sitting, no judging. The first thirty are everybody’s ideas; the last thirty are yours.
  2. The next day, circle five. Choose by asymmetry: cheap to try, no ceiling if it works. Write them on the ledger.
  3. Make the smallest version of one this week, and show it to one person outside your house. Then one a week for the rest of the month.
  4. Start the commonplace book. Any notebook. One page a day: sentences copied by hand, observations, connections. Date every page.
  5. Hold the ratio at one to one. An hour made for every hour consumed. Keep a rough tally on the ledger.

Why it works Hits scale with attempts; discernment needs candidates; each small thing shown is a loop closed. The commonplace book supplies the raw material that combination runs on.

Where it fails Editing during generation, and making the first idea instead of the hundredth. If the hundred took forty minutes, you stopped at the obvious ones. Do it again.
