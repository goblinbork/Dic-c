Notes & Sources

Where a chapter rests on research, the research is named here, with what it shows and what it does not. Effect sizes are given where they matter. A study of undergraduates recalling Swahili is evidence about memory, not a promise about your career; the book has tried to claim only what the evidence supports and to label a story as a story.

Before you begin

Andy Matuschak, “Why books don’t work” (2019), andymatuschak.org: the argument that books rest on an unstated model of learning, transmissionism, and that most readers retain little and rarely notice. On price and use: John Gourville and Dilip Soman, “Pricing and the Psychology of Consumption”, Harvard Business Review, September 2002, on health-club members whose attendance tracked how recently they had paid. Baba Shiv, Ziv Carmon and Dan Ariely, Journal of Marketing Research 42 (2005): people who paid a discounted price for an energy drink performed worse on puzzles than those who paid full price. The claim that an expensive book gets used is a claim about tendency, not a guarantee.

I · The Eulogy

Bronnie Ware, The Top Five Regrets of the Dying (Balboa Press, 2011; Hay House, 2012), from her 2009 essay “Regrets of the Dying”; the five regrets are paraphrased in her order. Daniel H. Pink, The Power of Regret (Riverhead, 2022): the World Regret Survey (more than 16,000 regrets from 105 countries at publication) and the American Regret Project (4,489 adults); the four core regrets; the finding that inaction regrets overtake action regrets with age, about two to one overall, building on Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec, Psychological Review 102 (1995). Jeff Bezos, interview with the Academy of Achievement, 4 May 2001, on the “regret minimization framework”. Hal Hershfield et al., “Increasing Saving Behavior Through Age-Progressed Renderings of the Future Self”, Journal of Marketing Research 48 (2011): participants who saw an aged image of themselves allocated $172 of a hypothetical $1,000 to retirement, against $80 for those who saw their present face. Prospective hindsight: see chapter IV. Steve Jobs, Stanford commencement address, 12 June 2005. The Nobel obituary story is widely repeated and unverified; no copy of the newspaper has been located, and it is presented as legend.

II · The Weeks

Oliver Burkeman, Four Thousand Weeks (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2021): “assuming you live to be eighty, you’ll have had about four thousand weeks.” The chart has ninety rows of fifty-two, with the eightieth year marked; eighty years of fifty-two weeks is 4,160, and counting days gives about 4,170. Tim Urban, “Your Life in Weeks” (2014) and “The Tail End” (2015), Wait But Why; the parental arithmetic in the chapter follows his method with illustrative numbers. Seneca, De Brevitate Vitae 1.3, in C. D. N. Costa’s translation (Penguin, 1997). Annie Dillard, The Writing Life (Harper & Row, 1989), p. 32. Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 2.11, in Gregory Hays’s translation (Modern Library, 2002); the Greek title, Ta eis heauton, means “to himself”.

III · The Question

Richard Hamming, “You and Your Research”, Bell Communications Research colloquium, 7 March 1986. Jacob Getzels and Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, The Creative Vision: A Longitudinal Study of Problem Finding in Art (Wiley, 1976): art students observed choosing and arranging objects before drawing; those who explored longer produced work rated more original, and problem-finding behaviour predicted professional success in follow-ups years later. The sample was small and the field was art; the chapter treats the result as suggestive, not settled. Rainer Maria Rilke, Letters to a Young Poet, letter of 16 July 1903, translated by M. D. Herter Norton (Norton, 1934). The “five whys”: Taiichi Ohno, Toyota Production System (1978; English 1988). Paul Graham, “How to Do Great Work” (July 2023), paulgraham.com. “What would have to be true” is Roger Martin’s formulation, in Playing to Win (with A. G. Lafley, Harvard Business Review Press, 2013). The term eigenquestion is used by Shishir Mehrotra. Leonardo’s lists of questions are in the Codex Atlanticus and are discussed in Walter Isaacson, Leonardo da Vinci (Simon & Schuster, 2017).

IV · Inversion

Charles T. Munger, “Prescriptions for Guaranteed Misery in Life”, commencement address, Harvard School, Los Angeles, 13 June 1986; the German maxim is traditionally attributed to Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi but has no located primary source. Deborah Mitchell, J. Edward Russo and Nancy Pennington, “Back to the Future: Temporal Perspective in the Explanation of Events”, Journal of Behavioral Decision Making 2 (1989): explaining an outcome as certain and past produced about thirty per cent more reasons, and more specific ones; the study measured the number and specificity of reasons, not their accuracy. Gary Klein, “Performing a Project Premortem”, Harvard Business Review, September 2007. Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross, “Exploring the ‘Planning Fallacy’”, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 67 (1994): thirty-seven honours students predicted 33.9 days for their theses (48.6 in the worst case) and took 55.5. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky named the planning fallacy in 1979. Annie Duke, Quit (Portfolio, 2022), on kill criteria as “states and dates”. The phrase “anti-goals” is Andrew Wilkinson’s. The 1935 crash of the Boeing Model 299 at Wright Field and the origin of the pilot’s checklist are recounted in Atul Gawande, The Checklist Manifesto (Metropolitan, 2009). Alex Haynes et al., “A Surgical Safety Checklist to Reduce Morbidity and Mortality in a Global Population”, New England Journal of Medicine 360 (2009): complications fell from 11.0 to 7.0 per cent and deaths from 1.5 to 0.8 per cent across eight hospitals.

V · Asymmetry

Jeff Bezos, 2015 Letter to Shareholders, Amazon.com (April 2016), on Type 1 and Type 2 decisions. Dean Keith Simonton, “Creative Productivity: A Predictive and Explanatory Model of Career Trajectories and Landmarks”, Psychological Review 104 (1997), and Scientific Genius (Cambridge, 1988): the equal-odds rule holds approximately, and later work (Forthmann et al., 2019) finds departures from it. The bounded-and-unbounded framing, the barbell and the idea of ruin as an absorbing state are Nassim Nicholas Taleb’s, in Antifragile (Random House, 2012) and Skin in the Game (2018). Annie Duke, Thinking in Bets (Portfolio, 2018), on “resulting”. Peter F. Drucker, “Managing Oneself”, Harvard Business Review, March–April 1999: “Whenever you make a key decision or take a key action, write down what you expect will happen. Nine or 12 months later, compare the actual results with your expectations.” Benjamin Franklin to Joseph Priestley, London, 19 September 1772, on “Moral or Prudential Algebra”. J. L. Kelly, “A New Interpretation of Information Rate”, Bell System Technical Journal 35 (1956); Edward O. Thorp, A Man for All Markets (Random House, 2017).

VI · The Loop

K. Anders Ericsson, Ralf Krampe and Clemens Tesch-Römer, “The Role of Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance”, Psychological Review 100 (1993); Ericsson’s correction of the “10,000-hour rule” is in Ericsson and Robert Pool, Peak (Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, 2016). Brooke Macnamara, David Hambrick and Frederick Oswald, “Deliberate Practice and Performance in Music, Games, Sports, Education, and Professions: A Meta-Analysis”, Psychological Science 25 (2014), eighty-eight studies: 26, 21, 18, 4 and under 1 per cent of variance explained respectively. The reading of the professional figure as a loop-length problem is the book’s, not the authors’. Jeffrey Karpicke and Henry Roediger III, “The Critical Importance of Retrieval for Learning”, Science 319 (2008): about 80 per cent recall after a week with repeated retrieval against about 36 per cent with repeated study. Nicholas Cepeda et al., Psychological Bulletin 132 (2006) and Psychological Science 19 (2008), on spacing. Philip Tetlock and Dan Gardner, Superforecasting (Crown, 2015); the “30 per cent better than intelligence analysts” figure was reported by David Ignatius, Washington Post, 1 November 2013, and is cited by Tetlock. Charles Goodhart’s observation dates from 1975 and was generalised by Marilyn Strathern in 1997. Richard Feynman, “Cargo Cult Science”, Caltech commencement, 1974. Atul Gawande, “Personal Best”, The New Yorker, 3 October 2011.

William James, The Principles of Psychology (Henry Holt, 1890), vol. 1, ch. XI, p. 402. Herbert Simon, “Designing Organizations for an Information-Rich World” (1971). Sophie Leroy, “Why Is It So Hard to Do My Work? The Challenge of Attention Residue When Switching Between Work Tasks”, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 109 (2009). VIII · Compounding

James Clear, Atomic Habits (Avery, 2018): 1.01365 = 37.78. The table in the chapter uses weekly rates compounded over 52-week years; the figures are rounded. Hershfield et al. (2011), see chapter I. Cal Newport, So Good They Can’t Ignore You (Business Plus, 2012), on career capital. Naval Ravikant, “How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)”, 31 May 2018: leverage from “capital, people, and products with no marginal cost of replication (code and media)”, the last two being “permissionless leverage”; the epigraph is from the same thread. The rule about never interrupting compounding unnecessarily is widely attributed to Charles Munger. The claim that permissionless leverage has grown by an order of magnitude since 2018 is the book’s own estimate. Buffett’s net worth by age is documented in public filings and in Alice Schroeder, The Snowball (Bantam, 2008). The Lindy effect is discussed in Nassim Nicholas Taleb, Antifragile.

Simonton (1997), see chapter V. Ira Glass, “Ira Glass on Storytelling”, part 3, Current TV (2009); the wording follows the standard transcript. Henri Poincaré, Science et Méthode (1908), translated by Francis Maitland (1914). John Locke, “A New Method of Making Common-Place-Books” (London, 1706; first published in French, 1686). X · The Ask

Richard Wiseman, The Luck Factor (Century, 2003) and “The Luck Factor”, Skeptical Inquirer 27 (2003): the newspaper exercise is a demonstration reported by the author, not a controlled trial. Francis Flynn and Vanessa Lake (now Bohns), “If You Need Help, Just Ask: Underestimating Compliance with Direct Requests for Help”, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 95 (2008): predicted numbers of people to approach were roughly double the actual numbers. Mark Granovetter, “The Strength of Weak Ties”, American Journal of Sociology 78 (1973): of fifty-four respondents who found jobs through a contact, 16.7 per cent saw the contact often, 55.6 per cent occasionally and 27.8 per cent rarely. Jon Jecker and David Landy, “Liking a Person as a Function of Doing Him a Favour”, Human Relations 22 (1969); Franklin’s account is in his Autobiography. Jia Jiang, Rejection Proof (Harmony, 2015). Steve Jobs told the Hewlett story in his 1994 interview with the Silicon Valley Historical Association: “Most people never pick up the phone and call. Most people never ask.”

XI · The Body

Hans Van Dongen, Greg Maislin, Janet Mullington and David Dinges, “The Cumulative Cost of Additional Wakefulness”, Sleep 26 (2003): forty-eight adults, fourteen days at four, six or eight hours in bed; six hours or less produced deficits “equivalent to up to 2 nights of total sleep deprivation”, and subjects were “largely unaware of these increasing cognitive deficits”. Kirk Erickson et al., “Exercise Training Increases Size of Hippocampus and Improves Memory”, PNAS 108 (2011): 120 adults aged 55–80; the walking group’s anterior hippocampus grew about 2 per cent over a year while the stretching group’s shrank about 1.4 per cent. Charles Hillman, Kirk Erickson and Arthur Kramer, “Be Smart, Exercise Your Heart”, Nature Reviews Neuroscience 9 (2008). Melis Yilmaz Balban et al., “Brief Structured Respiration Practices Enhance Mood and Reduce Physiological Arousal”, Cell Reports Medicine 4 (2023): 108 participants completed a month of five daily minutes; cyclic sighing improved positive affect more than mindfulness meditation. The morning-light prescription follows the consensus of circadian research rather than a single trial. Marily Oppezzo and Daniel Schwartz, “Give Your Ideas Some Legs”, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition 40 (2014): creative output rose by an average of 60 per cent while walking. None of this is medical advice.

XII · The Others

The Harvard Study of Adult Development began in 1938 with 268 Harvard sophomores and 456 boys from Boston’s poorest neighbourhoods. Robert Waldinger, “What Makes a Good Life?”, TEDxBeaconStreet, November 2015; Robert Waldinger and Marc Schulz, The Good Life (Simon & Schuster, 2023), on “social fitness”. Robin Dunbar, Journal of Human Evolution 22 (1992) and Friends (Little, Brown, 2021); a 2021 reanalysis by Lindenfors, Wartel and Lind found the confidence interval around 150 too wide to support a specific number, which the chapter notes. Martin Seligman, Tracy Steen, Nansook Park and Christopher Peterson, “Positive Psychology Progress: Empirical Validation of Interventions”, American Psychologist 60 (2005): the gratitude visit produced large positive change for one month; “three good things” increased happiness and decreased depressive symptoms for six months. Adam Grant, Give and Take (Viking, 2013). Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn, “Social Interactions and Well-Being: The Surprising Power of Weak Ties”, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 40 (2014). John Gottman, Why Marriages Succeed or Fail (Simon & Schuster, 1994) and, with Joan DeClaire, The Relationship Cure (Crown, 2001), on the five-to-one ratio and on turning toward bids. Bronnie Ware, see chapter I.

The Vow

Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography, part two: thirteen virtues, a page per virtue, seven columns, a black spot per fault, one virtue a week, four courses a year. Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran, “Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta-Analysis of Effects and Processes”, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38 (2006): ninety-four independent tests, more than eight thousand participants, d = 0.65. Jon Elster, Ulysses and the Sirens (Cambridge, 1979). James Clear, Atomic Habits, ch. 2. Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, book II. Two popular ideas are absent from this book on purpose: “grit” and “growth mindset”. Meta-analyses by Credé, Tynan and Harms (2017) and Sisk et al. (2018) found the first largely indistinguishable from conscientiousness and the second’s interventions to have very small effects. They may yet prove useful. The evidence is not strong enough to build a month on.
