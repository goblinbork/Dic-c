Instrument two · Time
II

The Weeks

A life is four thousand weeks. Spend them on purpose.

It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a lot of it.Seneca, On the Shortness of Life, c. 49 AD

Month two

If you live to eighty, you will have had a little over four thousand weeks. Written as a number the figure is not alarming; it is smaller than the population of a village. Drawn as a grid, fifty-two boxes wide and eighty rows tall, it fits comfortably on a single sheet of paper, and the box for this week is smaller than a fingernail. Tim Urban drew that grid in 2014 and Oliver Burkeman built a book on the arithmetic, and the reaction of almost everyone who sees it for the first time is the same: a short silence, and then a change in the way they hold their calendar.

Seneca, who was rich, busy and eventually executed, made the same point two thousand years earlier. The problem is not that life is short. The problem is that we treat time as if it were a fluid, arriving in an endless stream, when it is in fact a countable set of boxes, most of which are already coloured in. Nobody wastes a thing they can count. We waste time because we have never been made to see it.

This chapter comes with a chart. It is folded in the box the book came in, and this month it will be filled in with a pen. The chart is not a memento mori, or not only that. It is an instrument of scale, and its purpose is allocation, not dread.

Why it works

The week is the right unit for a life. A day is too small to plan anything of consequence, and a year is too large to feel; but a week is the cycle at which the important things actually recur. Work recurs weekly. Rest recurs weekly. The people you love, if you see them at all, are seen on a weekly rhythm or not seen. A habit that survives its first twelve weeks usually survives. Whatever you do every week, you do with your life; whatever you do not do in a given week, you have not done, and the box is coloured in regardless.

Making time countable does two things. It makes it finite, and finite things get allocated. And it makes the future self, who will run out of boxes, into someone specific, whose interests you can weigh against your present one. Annie Dillard put the whole argument into one sentence: how we spend our days is, of course, how we spend our lives. The chart is that sentence, drawn.

The practice is old. Marcus Aurelius, who ruled an empire and wrote at night for nobody but himself, kept returning to it: you could leave life right now, he wrote; let that determine what you do and say and think. The Romans did not find this morbid. They found it clarifying, in the way that a deadline is clarifying, and the chart is simply the deadline made visible, one small box at a time. It does not ask you to think about death. It asks you to think about Thursday.

A week is the unit of a life: small enough to plan, large enough to matter, and it comes around again.

The most useful sum on the chart is not the total. It is what Urban called the tail end. Suppose your parents are seventy and you see them three times a year, for a weekend each. If they live to eighty-five, you will see them about forty-five more times. If you left home at eighteen and saw them daily until then, you have already used more than nine tenths of the time you will ever spend in their company. Run the same sum for the friend in another city, the child who will leave in six years, the sibling. The numbers are not cruel. They are simply true, and they are the fastest way to decide what a week is for.

How to hold it

The chart produces two decisions. The first is a weekly non-negotiable: one block of at least two hours, at the same time every week, given to the thing your governing lines require and nothing else. It is not an aspiration; it is an appointment, and it goes in the calendar for the next twelve weeks before this chapter is finished. It will be interrupted. Defend it the way you would defend a flight you had paid for.

The second decision is a subtraction. Something recurs weekly in your life that you would not choose if it were offered fresh: a meeting, a standing obligation, a habit that fills the evening. Stop it. Not later, not after this month. The chart will show you why: a weekly hour is fifty hours a year, and fifty hours a year for the rest of a working life is more than two thousand hours, which is more than a year of work.

Look, too, at the rows already coloured. They are not the wasted part. They are the base the rest is built on, and the chart makes an argument that is easy to miss when the days are lived one at a time: a good week repeated is not one good week, it is a row, and rows are what a life is made of.

Some owners keep the chart alive after the month is over, and it is worth the minute it costs. On Sunday evening, colour the week just finished and write a single word in the box: the place, the person, the thing that happened. After a year the row is a diary of the kind that nobody has the discipline to keep in any other form, and after ten it is the only honest record of a decade that most people will ever have.

Hold the chart lightly after that. It is a map, not a countdown. People who stare at it become anxious, and anxiety spends weeks too. Fill it, do the two sums, make the two decisions, and then fold it and put it away until the next month asks you to open it. The boxes will keep being coloured in whether you watch or not. The only question the chart asks is what colour.

Practice · Instrument two

Fill the chart

One hour · the Weeks Chart · a pen, not a pencil · within the first seven days

  1. Colour every week you have lived. One row per year of your life, fifty-two boxes per row, from your birth to this week. Do it by hand; the hour it takes is part of the instrument.
  2. Draw a line at eighty. Count the rows between this week and the line. Write the number of weeks on the ledger.
  3. Run the tail-end sum for three people. Take the three people your eulogy would name. For each: how many times a year do you see them, and for how many more years? Multiply. Write the three numbers on the ledger, next to their names.
  4. Choose the weekly non-negotiable. Two hours or more, same day, same time, for the work your governing lines require. Write it on the chart’s legend, on the ledger, and in the calendar for the next twelve weeks.
  5. Choose one weekly recurrence to stop. Cancel it today. Write what it was and what it cost you per year.

Why it works Countable time gets allocated; uncounted time gets spent. The non-negotiable turns a governing line into a recurring appointment, which is the only form in which a value survives contact with a calendar.

Where it fails Filling the chart and making no decision. The chart is finished only when the calendar has twelve appointments in it and one recurring thing has been cancelled.
