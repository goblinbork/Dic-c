The Vow

Benjamin Franklin, at twenty, made himself a small book with a page for each of thirteen virtues and seven columns for the days of the week, and every evening he marked with a black spot each fault against the virtue of the week. A course took thirteen weeks; he ran four a year; and he kept the book, on and off, for the rest of his life. He never reached the perfection he was aiming at, he wrote, and was a better and happier man for the attempt. It is the earliest instrument of this kind we know of in English, and the twelve ledgers you have filled are its descendants.

The year has rested on one of the best-replicated findings in psychology. Intentions of the form I will exercise more change almost nothing. Intentions of the form when it is seven on Tuesday, I will put on my shoes and walk to the park change a great deal: across ninety-four tests and more than eight thousand people, tying an action to a specific situation had a medium-to-large effect, and it held for goals people had already failed at. Peter Gollwitzer calls them implementation intentions. The situation does the remembering, so the person does not have to.

There is an older word for a decision made now that binds you later. Ulysses had himself tied to the mast so that he could hear the sirens without steering towards them; the philosopher Jon Elster called it precommitment. Ulysses did not trust the man he would be in an hour, and he was wise enough to arrange things while he was still the man who could. The kill criterion in the fourth month was a rope of this kind. So is the page opposite.

Write one vow. Not twelve, not a list: one sentence, in the form the research recommends, tying a situation you will certainly meet to an action you have already tested this year, in the name of the person the eulogy described and your ledgers say you have begun to be. Every action, as James Clear puts it, is a vote for the kind of person you wish to become. Sign it. If a witness is available, have them sign it too. Then close the book, and open the envelope.
